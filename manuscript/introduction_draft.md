# Introduction Draft

Protein-coding missense variants are common in human genetic data, but many are
difficult to interpret. A missense variant changes one amino acid in a protein.
Some such changes are harmless, while others disrupt protein function, alter
protein interactions, or increase disease risk. For many disease-associated
genes, the number of possible missense variants is much larger than the number of
variants that have been experimentally or clinically characterized. This creates
a practical gap: clinicians and researchers often see variants whose biological
importance is uncertain.

MAPT encodes the microtubule-associated protein Tau, a neuronal protein involved
in microtubule binding and stabilization. Tau is also central to a group of
neurodegenerative diseases known as tauopathies, where abnormal Tau pathology is
a defining feature. In inherited frontotemporal dementia and parkinsonism linked
to chromosome 17, missense and splice-site mutations in MAPT provided direct
genetic evidence that Tau dysfunction can cause neurodegeneration
[@hutton1998tau; @spillantini2000tau].

The biological interpretation of MAPT variation is complicated by Tau isoforms.
Adult human brain expresses multiple Tau isoforms generated by alternative
splicing. These isoforms differ in N-terminal inserts and in the number of
microtubule-binding repeats. The 441-amino-acid 2N4R Tau-F / hTau40 isoform is a
commonly used reference for Tau biology, but external databases may report MAPT
variants using different transcript or protein coordinate systems. A variant that
appears to have the same position number may therefore not always refer to the
same amino-acid context. This makes strict coordinate checking essential before
combining clinical labels, external predictor scores, and protein-model outputs
[@spillantini2000tau].

Large public resources such as ClinVar collect clinical interpretations of human
variants and are useful for benchmarking computational predictions
[@landrum2018clinvar]. However, ClinVar is not a complete truth set. Many
variants remain variants of uncertain significance, and submitted interpretations
can be sparse, conflicting, or dependent on the reference sequence used. For
MAPT/Tau-F, strict reference-residue matching leaves only a small binary benchmark
of clearly pathogenic/likely pathogenic versus benign/likely benign missense
variants. This limits the strength of conventional performance claims based only
on AUROC or AUPRC.

Protein language models offer a complementary approach. These models are trained
on large collections of natural protein sequences and learn statistical patterns
that reflect evolutionary and biochemical constraints. Prior work showed that
large protein language models can encode information related to protein structure
and function from sequence data alone [@rives2021biological]. ESM-1v further
demonstrated that pretrained protein language models can be used in a zero-shot
setting to predict mutation effects without task-specific experimental training
data [@meier2021language]. In this setting, a model can score whether a mutant
amino acid is more or less compatible with the learned protein sequence context.

Zero-shot scoring is attractive for MAPT because it does not require a large
Tau-specific training set. Instead of training a disease classifier from scarce
clinical labels, the model provides a label-free prior over all possible missense
substitutions. This is especially useful for generating a complete variant atlas:
every possible single amino-acid change can be scored before clinical labels are
used for interpretation. The resulting atlas can then be examined by Tau region,
known disease hotspots, aggregation motifs, post-translational modification
proximity, and clinical annotation status.

General missense predictors such as AlphaMissense provide another useful point of
comparison. AlphaMissense predicts pathogenicity for human missense variants by
combining structural context and evolutionary information and provides
proteome-wide scores as a community resource [@cheng2023alphamissense]. However,
direct use of such external scores still requires careful coordinate QC for genes
with multiple isoforms, including MAPT. For Tau-F, AlphaMissense is therefore
best treated as an external reference and coverage check unless its coordinates
can be fully matched to the chosen atlas reference.

Here, we present a reproducible zero-shot missense atlas for 441-aa Tau-F. We
enumerated all 8379 possible single amino-acid substitutions, scored them with an
ESM-1v ensemble, added Tau-specific mechanistic annotations, and integrated
ClinVar and AlphaMissense through strict reference-residue QC. We focus on a
conservative manuscript claim: the atlas provides a transparent, reproducible
framework for MAPT/Tau missense variant prioritization, domain-level analysis,
and VUS follow-up, rather than a standalone clinical diagnostic model.

## Plain-Language Structure

For a non-specialist reader, the story is:

1. Tau is an important brain protein.
2. Some Tau mutations can cause serious neurodegenerative disease.
3. There are thousands of possible Tau mutations, but only a few are well known.
4. We used a pretrained protein AI model to score every possible single Tau-F
   missense mutation.
5. We carefully checked clinical databases so that coordinates were not mixed up.
6. The result is a Tau mutation priority map, not a clinical diagnosis tool.
